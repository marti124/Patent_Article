Nineteen Groups, One Wall

The incandescent bulb is one spoke on a wheel. The rule that took it off the shelf reaches eighteen other groups of Americans, for the same reason and in the same way.

By Roleigh Martin

A patent lasts for a “limited Time.” When it ends, the design belongs to the public and anyone may build it again. That is the bargain the Constitution struck in 1787, two and a half years before Congress passed the first patent law.

Since about 1970, federal rules have required that a remake of an old design meet the standards written for new designs — which a faithful remake can rarely do without ceasing to be that design. The patent ends on schedule; the exclusion outlives it. The bulb is the case a reader can see in a kitchen. It is not the only one.

The nineteen

Each has a concrete, present-day injury and, the book argues, standing to bring it. Seven are sovereign or public-fiduciary plaintiffs; twelve are direct and associational.

Sovereign and public-fiduciary

  • State attorneys general — 50 states; every state fleet overpays
  • Indian tribes — 345 tribal nations; the burden falls hardest on reservation economies
  • Governors — procurement of patrol cars, plows and coaches
  • Sheriffs — 3,091 elected sheriffs, overpaying on every patrol vehicle
  • School districts — about 13,000, buying bus fleets on a public budget line
  • Public transit authorities — 2,000-plus agencies; $1.5 million and up per coach
  • Volunteer fire departments — about 19,000; engines from $250,000 to $750,000

Direct and associational

  • Healthy-lighting medical groups — 3.4 million with epilepsy alone; those who cannot use LEDs lost a century-old bulb
  • Hospitals — some 6,000; sterilizers, laundries, HVAC and food service
  • Farming associations — 1.9 million farms; tractors and combines at multiples of the Indian price
  • Coin-laundry operators — 30,000 storefronts, each a room of federally covered washers
  • Restaurant owner associations — 380,000 locations; a dozen kitchen-equipment categories
  • Hotel and lodging operators — 60,000 properties; the cost hides in the room rate
  • Apartment and multi-family owners — 95,000 companies; HVAC and water heaters at every turnover
  • Construction contractor associations — 190,000 firms buying Tier 4 equipment
  • Earth-moving equipment owners — patents expired, Tier 4 blocks the remake; the widest price gap of all
  • Truckers and owner-operators — about 350,000; a rig costs more than a house
  • Rideshare and taxicab drivers — 1.7 million buying the regulated vehicle that is the whole business
  • Landscaping operators — 100,000 professionals; mowers and blowers under EPA Part 1054

Why a wheel, and not a list

Because the injury is identical. A hospital replacing lamps, a dairy farm keeping a thirty-year-old tractor alive, a laundry that cannot buy a new machine built to a design its own patents released decades ago, and a household that can no longer buy the bulb its grandmother bought — all four are told the same thing by the same rule: the design is public, and you still may not have it made.

That shared injury is what makes the question answerable in one courtroom rather than nineteen. No federal court has yet been asked it.
